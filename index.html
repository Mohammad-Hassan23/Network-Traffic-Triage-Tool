Privacy

Your packet capture data is analysed locally in your browser and is not uploaded anywhere.

This page has no analytics, no cookies, no tracking, no advertising, no telemetry and no external requests of any kind. The CSV you select is read into this tab's memory, analysed by JavaScript that runs on your own machine, and discarded when you close or refresh the page. There is no server, no database and no API behind this tool. You can confirm all of this yourself: open your browser's developer tools, switch to the Network tab, and load a capture. You will see this page's own files and nothing else.

    7How to Think Like an Analyst

    The tool does the counting. You do the thinking. This is the workflow a defender follows after any alert, in this tool or in a real SOC.

    1. Observe unusual behaviour. Something stands out: a host far busier than its peers, a lot of connection attempts, a burst of name lookups.
    2. Identify the source and destination. Which device is responsible, and who is it talking to? A name or asset owner turns an IP address into a story.
    3. Determine which protocol is involved. DNS, ICMP, SMB, TLS and HTTP all fail in different ways. The protocol narrows down what the traffic could be.
    4. Establish whether the activity is expected. Is this host a backup server, a scanner, a printer, a monitoring agent? Expected behaviour is not a finding.
    5. Look for supporting indicators. Volume alone is weak evidence. Volume plus timing plus a strange destination plus a service that should not be reachable is stronger.
    6. Correlate with other security telemetry. Endpoint logs, authentication events, firewall logs and change records answer questions packet headers cannot.
    7. Decide whether further investigation is required. Document the outcome either way. "Expected backup traffic, confirmed with the asset owner" is a valid and valuable conclusion.

    An anomaly is a starting point for investigation, not proof of compromise.

    Detection tells an analyst where to look. Investigation determines what actually happened.

    8Capturing and exporting with Wireshark

    Only capture traffic from systems and networks you own or are authorised to monitor. Capturing other people's traffic may be illegal where you live and can breach policies at work or school. When in doubt, use the demo data in this tool instead.

    1. Open Wireshark and select the network interface you are authorised to monitor.
    2. Start the capture and generate the traffic you want to study (browse a page, ping a host, run an authorised scan against your own lab).
    3. Stop the capture.
    4. Export the packet list: File → Export Packet Dissections → As CSV…, then choose a destination file. Keep the capture short (a few minutes) so the numbers stay readable.
    5. Upload that CSV here with Upload Wireshark CSV, or drag it onto the upload box.

    Tip: a smaller capture tells a clearer story than a one-hour capture. Ten minutes of focused traffic is usually enough for the rules in this tool to be meaningful.

    9What this tool cannot tell you

    Knowing the limits of a tool is part of using it competently. These are the honest boundaries of a CSV-based triage helper.

    • A CSV holds less than a capture file. Wireshark can show flags, options, payloads, response times and TLS details. The packet-list CSV export does not, so some questions simply cannot be answered from this input.
    • Encrypted traffic hides its contents. You can see that two hosts exchanged 1.4 KB segments, but not whether it was a backup, a video, or something else.
    • Packet volume is not a measure of badness. A nightly backup is 100,000 packets of entirely good news.
    • Thresholds vary enormously by environment. A domain controller, a build server and a laptop all look wildly different while behaving normally.
    • NAT and proxies blur host attribution. Hundreds of devices behind one public address appear as a single very busy host.
    • The whole capture is treated as one time window. A 10-minute and a 6-hour capture of identical traffic produce very different counts, so thresholds are not comparable across captures of different lengths.
    • Protocol labels come from Wireshark. Values such as TLSv1.2, QUIC or SSDP are mapped into simple families for readability, and anything unrecognised is grouped under Other.
    • Port and TCP-flag detection depends on the Info column. If your export does not contain recognisable port or flag text, Rules 3 and 7 are reported as not evaluated rather than guessed.
    • This is not malware analysis. It never inspects payloads, hashes or files.
    • It does not replace Wireshark, an IDS, a SIEM or professional network monitoring. It is a teaching aid for the reasoning step of triage.
